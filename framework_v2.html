An Evaluative Framework for
AI Deployment Decisions

Middlesex School  ·  Concord, Massachusetts

School AI policies establish that appropriate AI use is discipline-specific and determined by departments and teachers, but they offer no structure for how teachers make those determinations. This framework provides structure to those conversations.

Teachers should hold three considerations in mind when evaluating any AI use — their own or their students'.

T
Teacher
What does this tool use do for the teacher — and what does it ask of them?
Deployment judgment Expertise required to decide whether and how to use AI for a task — exercised before use begins.
Teacher in-use agency The professional authority the teacher retains while the tool runs — whether their judgment is still shaping what students experience, or whether the system has taken over the meaningful decisions.
L
Learner
What does this AI use do for the student — and can they reasonably exercise the judgment it requires?
Proximity to learning How directly the AI touches the core acts of instruction, learning, feedback, and assessment.
Student judgment burden The degree of critical judgment students must independently supply for this AI use to go well — not as a condition of access, but as a requirement for the interaction to be educationally sound.
C
Capacities
What capacities does this AI use build in students?
Tool fluency Whether sustained use builds AI literacy — understanding how these systems behave and how to work with them critically.
Disciplinary knowledge Whether sustained use builds students' content understanding and disciplinary thinking.

Shape, not score

Together, the dimensions described above can help profile a particular AI use. They can be mapped to the visual profile here — select a consideration to see which dimensions it corresponds to.

A profile full in the upper half and thin in the lower means significant teacher judgment is required and the AI's reach into student experience is limited — the professional is doing the work. Heavy in the lower half with a thin upper means the AI touches instruction, feedback, or assessment directly, while judgment lands on the student rather than the teacher.

The equator tracks student capacities over time. Both horizontal dimensions trace what sustained use builds or erodes in students. Whether high or low serves your students depends on your curricular goals.

The aim is not to encourage AI uses with high totals. It is to surface what a specific use actually asks of teachers and students — so that judgment can be exercised deliberately.

See the framework in action. Two concrete examples — one from English, one from mathematics — show how the same three questions produce very different profiles for very different AI uses.
Case study 1 · English
The Discussion Guide

An English teacher is preparing for a class discussion on Beloved. She opens a conversation with ChatGPT and describes the passage students will have read, the grade level, and her learning goal: she wants students to move from surface-level plot questions toward questions about narrative voice and historical memory. She asks ChatGPT to generate a tiered set of discussion questions organized from literal to interpretive to evaluative, then spends twenty minutes working through the output — cutting questions that feel generic, rewriting two that are close but miss the specificity of Morrison's language, and adding three of her own that she knows will resonate with this particular class. Students receive a discussion guide she's authored around the final set of questions.

Teacher
Deployment judgment Substantial — she had to know what a good discussion question looks like, understand her students, and frame the prompt so that the output was worth editing rather than discarding. Teacher in-use agency High throughout. She is the author; the AI is subordinate to her editorial judgment at every step. The final product reflects her knowledge of the text and of her students — neither is replaceable.
Learner
Proximity to learning Low. Students never encounter the AI.
Student judgment burden Not applicable. The AI's presence in the room is zero.
Capacities
Tool fluency Accrues to the teacher, not to students, in this use.
Disciplinary knowledge Served, not bypassed. A teacher who doesn't know Beloved can't edit the questions — knowing the text is what the tool requires of her.
Profile: Heavy upper half, negligible lower half. AI in the teacher's workshop. Students experience a better-designed lesson.
Case study 2 · Mathematics
The Justification App

A math teacher builds a structured web activity that pairs an interactive graph with a constrained AI chat window of his own design. The AI acts as a skeptical peer — one that pushes back on a student's reasoning until the argument is genuinely airtight. The activity unfolds in three phases deliberately targeting the upper levels of Bloom's taxonomy: analyzing a claim, evaluating a peer's argument, and creating new prompts for future students.

With the help of AI, he builds a question bank of mathematical claims and designs a rubric the AI will use to assess student responses. Rather than a simple chat interface, the application packages each student response together with the teacher's pre-written instructions before sending it to the AI — which is how he controls what the AI can and cannot do. The AI cannot reveal its rubric or simply give the answer.

Teacher
Deployment judgment Very high — exercised before students opened the app. The teacher had to understand his students' specific reasoning gaps, design scaffolding that closes those gaps without bypassing them, and build constraints that make the AI behave as needed. That expertise is encoded in the tool itself. Teacher in-use agency Lower at run time — the structure does the work. But the structure is his judgment, made durable. His agency was exercised at design; it is present in the architecture.
Learner
Proximity to learning High. The AI is directly inside the student's reasoning process across all three phases.
Student judgment burden High — but demand by design, not demand by default. The student cannot skip the reasoning; the tool is built to require it.
Capacities
Tool fluency Moderate. Students learn to reason with a tool that questions rather than answers — a specific and transferable AI literacy.
Disciplinary knowledge The target. Take the app away — what remains is the argument the student constructed. That is the test.
Profile: High across most dimensions, with the lower half active but structured. AI is present in the student experience because the teacher placed it there deliberately, with constraints a general-purpose tool would not have.
